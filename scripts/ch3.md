## S3.1 · Seven hexagons
`id: s3_1` · `target: 1:30` · `time: 9:18–10:42 (est.)`

**NARRATION**
> Go back to hexagons, but this time use seven colours. [beat] Make each hexagon a bit less than one across: a radius of two fifths, so its widest distance is zero point eight. Then no two points inside one hexagon are a unit apart. [beat] Now colour them in a repeating pattern: each hexagon is surrounded by six neighbours, all different colours, making a flower of seven. Tile the plane with these flowers, offset just right. [beat] In this pattern, hexagons of the same colour are always far apart. The nearest points of two same-coloured hexagons are a little more than one apart, at least about one point zero three. [beat] So any stick either stays inside one hexagon, too short to matter, or reaches a hexagon of a different colour. Seven colours suffice, even counting the borders, which can go to either side. [beat2] So: at least four, at most seven. Edward Nelson posed the question in 1950 and saw the four. John Isbell found the seven-colour hexagons the same year. Hugo Hadwiger published the problem in 1961, the year of the Moser spindle, and it became known as the Hadwiger–Nelson problem. [beat] And then the number line didn't move, for sixty-eight years.

## S3.2 · Every infinite answer has a finite witness
`id: s3_2` · `target: 1:10` · `time: 10:42–11:58 (est.)`

**NARRATION**
> Let's think about what a better lower bound would have to look like. Suppose the plane really needs five colours. Must there be a finite drawing, like the spindle, that proves it? [beat] A theorem from 1951, by Nicolaas de Bruijn and Paul Erdős, says yes. If every finite piece of a graph can be properly coloured with k colours, then the whole infinite graph can be too. [beat] Picture finite patches, each with its own valid colouring. The theorem guarantees the choices can be made consistently, so that they lock together into one colouring of everything. [beat] Turn that around. If the plane needs five colours, then somewhere there is a finite unit-distance graph that needs five. The same goes for six. [beat] So the hunt for finite graphs always has a target, at least in principle. The catch: the theorem doesn't say how big that graph is, or where to look. [beat] One more detail to keep in your pocket. This theorem relies on the axiom of choice, a standard ingredient of modern mathematics, and it lets colourings be very strange indeed.

## S3.3 · 2018
`id: s3_3` · `target: 2:20` · `time: 11:58–14:09 (est.)`

**NARRATION**
> For decades the search for a five-colour graph went nowhere. Then, in April 2018, Aubrey de Grey posted a paper with a blunt title: "The chromatic number of the plane is at least 5". [beat] His approach was to build up from spindle-like pieces into larger structures that restrict how they can be coloured. His first graph had twenty thousand, four hundred and twenty-five vertices, and he checked its key property with a program he wrote himself. Then he shrank it, down to one thousand five hundred and eighty-one. [beat] How do you check that a graph with fifteen hundred vertices can't be four-coloured? There are four to the power fifteen hundred possible colourings: a number with more than nine hundred digits. You can't list them. [beat] Instead, you translate the question into logic. For each vertex, a variable for each possible colour. For each edge, a constraint: not the same colour at both ends. Then you hand the whole thing to a SAT solver, a program built to decide whether constraints like these can all be satisfied at once. [beat] When the answer is no, a modern solver doesn't just say "no". It can write down a refutation: a long list of small logical steps, each easy to check, ending in a plain contradiction. That list is a certificate. And checking it doesn't require trusting the solver, only a much simpler checking program. [beat] Within days, an online collaboration called Polymath sixteen formed to shrink and simplify these graphs. Marijn Heule used exactly this solver-and-certificate pipeline to find five-chromatic graphs with five hundred and fifty-three vertices. His certificates ran to between fourteen and nineteen thousand steps, checkable in about a second. Jaan Parts later reached five hundred and nine vertices, and also gave a proof that a human can verify by hand. [beat] For the first time in sixty-eight years, the number line moved. At least five. At most seven.
